Screened at every single visit

You just had major surgery. Someone clinical should be checking on you — not a pamphlet.
  • Blood pressure, heart rate and oxygen saturation
  • Incision site — redness, warmth, drainage, separation
  • Signs of blood clot (DVT) — calf pain, swelling, warmth
  • Shortness of breath or chest pain
  • Postpartum preeclampsia — headache, vision changes, swelling
  • Heavy bleeding or signs of infection
If something looks wrong, you get a same-day referral and a call to your surgeon. This is the layer of care a six-week checkup was never designed to provide.
Know Before You Go Home

Warning signs to watch for before I ever get there.

Most serious complications after a cesarean show up in the first days and weeks — often after you've already been discharged. You should not have to guess what's normal. Here is what to watch for in the hospital and at home, before our first visit.

Call 911 immediately

Do not wait, do not drive yourself, and do not wait for an appointment.

  • Chest pain
  • Trouble breathing or shortness of breath
  • Seizures
  • Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, you can also call or text 988 for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time of day. This is more common than most people are told, and it is treatable. You are not a bad parent for feeling it.

Call your provider the same day

Your OB, midwife or surgeon — not a message left for next week.

  • Fever of 100.4°F or higher
  • Heavy bleeding — soaking through a pad in an hour, or passing clots the size of an egg or larger
  • Incision changes — increasing redness, warmth, swelling, drainage, foul smell, or edges pulling apart
  • One red, swollen, warm or painful leg — especially the calf. This can be a blood clot
  • A headache that won't go away with medication, or a bad headache with vision changes or swelling in your face and hands
  • Pain that is getting worse rather than better, or pain not controlled by your prescribed medication
  • Unable to urinate, burning with urination, or no bowel movement with a hard, distended belly

Before you leave the hospital, ask

You are allowed to ask every one of these. Write the answers down.

  • What lifting restriction am I under, and for how many weeks?
  • How do I care for my incision, and when can it get wet?
  • How much bleeding is normal for me specifically?
  • What pain medication am I on, on what schedule, and for how long?
  • When should I start walking, and how much? Early movement helps prevent blood clots
  • Who exactly do I call after hours, and what is that number?
  • When is my follow-up, and do I need an earlier incision check?

The first days at home

What to pay attention to while you wait for our first visit.

  • Take your temperature once a day. Write it down
  • Look at your incision daily, with a mirror or a second set of eyes. Photograph it so you can compare
  • Track your bleeding — it should get lighter, not heavier. Going backwards is worth a call
  • Move a little, often. Short, slow walks around the house help your lungs, bowels and circulation
  • Do not lift more than your surgeon allowed, even if you feel fine
  • Watch your mood. Sadness, anxiety, rage, or feeling disconnected lasting more than two weeks deserves attention and help
  • Keep someone with you for the first stretch if you possibly can

This page is general education, not medical advice, and it does not replace the instructions your own surgical team gave you. When something feels wrong, trust that instinct and call. You are never being dramatic by checking.

FAQ

Questions, answered.

How soon after a C-section can we start?
Within days of your discharge home. Early, appropriate movement is one of the strongest predictors of a complete recovery, and the first weeks are when guidance matters most. Everything we do follows your surgeon's protocol.
Can we start before my scheduled cesarean?
Yes, and it's the best way to do this. I come to your home weeks before your due date and prepare both you and your caregivers — activity modification, how your partner or support people can help, equipment to have ready, and what the first two weeks will actually involve. You go into surgery prepared instead of improvising afterward.
Do you take insurance?
Resilient Bloom is a cash pay practice. You pay directly, with no authorizations and no delays. Superbills are provided for out-of-network reimbursement, and HSA and FSA cards are accepted.
Do I need a referral from my doctor?
No. North Carolina is a direct-access state, so you can book physical therapy directly. Care is always coordinated with your surgeon's protocol, and I'm glad to communicate with your provider.
What happens at the first visit?
The first visit happens at your home, weeks before your due date, and it covers three things. Preparing for surgery — what to expect, what to ask your surgical team, and how to build strength and mobility beforehand. Preparing to come home — setting up your space, equipment to have on hand, lifting and activity limits, and training your partner or caregivers on exactly how to help. And what you can safely do right away after surgery — breathing and circulation work, how to get in and out of bed without bracing your incision, how to protect it when you cough or laugh, and comfortable positions for feeding and holding your baby. You finish with a written plan so you aren't trying to remember any of it in the hospital.
And what happens at the visit after I come home?
Vital signs, post-surgical red flag screening, a look at your incision, and a movement assessment covering bed mobility, transfers and stairs — plus a check of how your core and pelvic floor are responding. We talk through how the birth actually went and what's hurting. You finish with a plan matched to your stage of recovery and your surgeon's protocol, and things you can start that same day.
What if I had a vaginal birth, not a cesarean?
You're welcome here. Cesarean recovery is the specialty, but every postpartum body benefits from pelvic floor and core rehabilitation — and tearing, episiotomy and prolonged pushing all have specific recovery needs.
Can my baby be there during the visit?
Absolutely. That's the entire point of coming to you. Feed, hold and settle your baby as you need to — we work around it, because that's your real life.
What areas do you serve?
Asheville, NC. We'll confirm your address during your free consultation.
